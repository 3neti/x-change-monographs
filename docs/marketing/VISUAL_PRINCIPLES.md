# Marketing Visual Principles

## Purpose

These principles define how the x-change Marketing Design System thinks. They govern the meaning and judgment behind illustrations without prescribing a drawing style, format, or renderer.

The visual language exists to make a human financial experience understandable before the reader consciously begins reading.

## The human outcome comes first

Illustrate people before technology.

Illustrate intention before infrastructure.

Illustrate outcomes before mechanics.

The Pay Code is never the hero. The human outcome is.

A composition should begin with what a person wants to accomplish: send, share, choose, claim, or receive. Institutional and technical elements appear only when they make that human action more truthful or easier to understand.

## The illustration explains

The illustration explains. The copy confirms.

Every primary illustration must remain understandable without its caption. A caption may name an action or confirm a distinction. It must not repair a confusing image.

When a visual requires a paragraph to be understood, simplify the visual rather than expanding the paragraph.

## One illustration, one idea

Each illustration answers one human question.

Examples include:

- Why do I not need the recipient's account details upfront?
- Where does the Pay Code come from?
- How does it reach the recipient?
- How does the recipient choose?
- Who remains responsible for moving the money?

Do not combine unrelated questions merely because a format has available space.

## Prefer verbs

Prefer verbs over nouns.

Prefer movement over objects.

`Generate`, `Share`, and `Claim` are memorable because they describe what people do. A cluster of objects—bank, phone, QR, cloud, wallet—does not become a story until their roles and movement are clear.

Objects support the action. They do not replace it.

## Preserve the canonical journey

The public visual story is:

```text
Generate
    ↓
Share
    ↓
Claim
```

VIS-001 is the canonical composition of this journey. Future publications reference and adapt VIS-001 rather than inventing a competing process.

Authorization remains inside Generate. Recipient Choice, presentation, validation, and completion remain inside Claim. They must not become additional first-level steps.

## Make choice bounded and singular

Recipient Choice is discovered during Claim.

One Pay Code may lead to a small set of available supported destinations. The recipient selects one. The visual must then return to one path and one completion.

One Pay Code must never appear to split into multiple payouts, create duplicated value, or complete simultaneously through several providers.

## Keep the credential honest

The Pay Code is a portable credential for an authorized instruction.

Its canonical visual identity is human-readable and portable: an uppercase alphanumeric code framed by two parallel-line marks, such as `|| 7K9M-4R2P ||`. The same legible code remains recognizable through Generate, Share, and Claim.

QR is an optional machine-readable encoding or sharing form. It may appear as a smaller companion or one channel among SMS, chat, email, print, and voice. It must not replace, exceed, or become the identity of the alphanumeric credential.

It must never visually contain:

- money;
- a balance;
- stored value;
- a deposit;
- a wallet; or
- a guaranteed outcome.

The artwork must not imply that scanning, a camera, or a smartphone is required.

Money does not travel through the communication channel. The credential does. Participating financial institutions and providers perform the financial service.

## Let institutions remain quiet and real

Institutional responsibility must be visible without dominating the human story.

Use governed frames, checkpoints, supported destinations, and evidence traces to preserve authority and accountability. Avoid turning the illustration into a legal, regulatory, settlement, or system-architecture diagram.

x-change may appear as an operating layer only when the composition answers an institutional question. It must never replace the responsible institution or participating financial provider.

## Prefer timeless metaphors

Prefer human figures, paths, credentials, gates, choices, destinations, receipts, and institutional boundaries.

Avoid:

- screenshots;
- vendor-specific interfaces;
- device fashions that quickly date;
- institution logos used as generic actors;
- decorative dashboards;
- unexplained technical infrastructure;
- visual effects that imply speed, wealth, or inevitability; and
- ornament that does not improve understanding.

A future renderer may change line, form, color, or medium. The metaphor should remain intelligible.

## Use detail only when it earns attention

Primary actions receive the strongest visual weight. Conditions, approval, validation, evidence, and settlement remain secondary unless the illustration exists specifically to explain one of them.

Complexity should be nested, not promoted.

When removing a detail does not change the viewer's understanding, remove it.

## Preserve dignity

People should appear capable, intentional, and specific enough to be understood without becoming stereotypes.

Avoid demographic caricature, desperation imagery, faceless crowds, exaggerated celebration, or depictions that reduce a recipient to a compliance object. Public-purpose and emergency-assistance illustrations must preserve the same dignity as commercial scenarios.

## Design for recognition across media

The same semantic relationships must survive print, web, presentations, displays, social cards, and motion.

A renderer may:

- rearrange horizontal frames vertically;
- reduce secondary detail;
- reveal a composition over time;
- crop governed whitespace; or
- adapt typography and scale.

It may not change actor roles, arrow meaning, sequence, institutional boundaries, or the credential-versus-money distinction.

## Evaluation tests

### The Question Test

Can a viewer state the single question the illustration answers?

If several unrelated answers compete, divide the composition.

### The Caption Test

Does the illustration remain understandable when its caption disappears?

If not, simplify the illustration.

### The Fifteen-Second Test

Can someone walking past understand the intended idea before reaching the next conference booth?

If not, reduce visual complexity.

### The Memory Test

After seeing the composition once, can the viewer later sketch:

```text
Generate
    ↓
Share
    ↓
Claim
```

The sketch does not need artistic accuracy. It must preserve the three actions, their order, and Recipient Choice inside Claim.

If the viewer remembers icons but not the journey, the visual language is too object-driven. If the viewer adds extra first-level steps, the hierarchy is too complicated.

### The Doctrine Test

Could the illustration reasonably be mistaken for stored value, universal acceptance, x-change custody, money creation, or duplicated redemption?

If yes, the concept is not ready regardless of its visual appeal.

## Approval principle

A successful x-change illustration feels simple because its meaning is disciplined, not because important truths were removed.

Visual appeal may invite attention. Semantic clarity earns trust.
